How the value score is calculated

Most "best battery" lists quietly default to the most expensive option on the shelf. PowerRank does the opposite on purpose: price is one of the inputs to the score, not an afterthought. Every listed spec earns points, then a typical selling price is subtracted. A model with great specs at a high price and a model with modest specs at a low price can land in exactly the same place — that's the point. The score tells you where the balance falls, not just which one has the biggest number on the box.

Capacity

Weighted on a curve, not a straight line (capacity0.585) — so jumping from 500 Wh to 1,000 Wh is worth more to the score than jumping from 2,000 Wh to 2,500 Wh. The first bump solves real problems; later bumps are diminishing returns for most people.

Ports & outlets

Each AC outlet, 120V/L2 port, and USB port adds a fixed amount, reflecting day-to-day usefulness — more things you can plug in at once without an adapter.

Peak output

Scored relative to the "max power needed" slider, not in isolation. A unit only earns credit for headroom up to what you said you need — wattage you'll never use doesn't inflate the score.

Feature bonuses

Expandable capacity, companion app support, jump-starting, MagSafe, built-in cables, a display, and a built-in wall plug each add a modest, fixed bonus reflecting real convenience.

Price

A typical selling price we research periodically is subtracted from everything above. A positive score means the spec sheet outweighs the price; negative means the price is outrunning what you get. Actual prices change often, so always check the current price before buying — it may shift a model's real-world value.

This is one opinionated formula, not an objective truth — the exact weights are judgment calls, and reasonable people could weigh capacity vs. ports vs. price differently depending on how they'll use a battery. Buying guide

How much capacity do I need?

Add up the watts of what you want to run and multiply by the hours you need. A rough runtime estimate is capacity (Wh) × 0.85 ÷ load (W) — the 0.85 accounts for inverter losses. For example, a 1,000 Wh station running a 60 W fridge averages around 14 hours. For whole-day backup of several devices, most people land between 1,000 and 2,000 Wh.

What's the difference between peak output and continuous output?

Continuous (rated) output is what the inverter can supply indefinitely. Peak or surge output is a brief burst for motors and compressors that draw extra power when they start. Size the continuous rating to your largest device, and make sure the surge rating covers startup spikes for things like fridges, pumps, and power tools. The "max power needed" slider lets you tell the score how much output actually matters to you.

What do L2, 120V, and USB outlets mean in the table?

120V is the standard household AC outlet. L2 counts 240V-capable or high-amp outlets (such as TT-30 or L14-30 connectors) used for RVs, EVs, and transfer switches — only larger stations have them. USB counts USB-A and USB-C ports combined.

Why are power banks rated in mAh instead of Wh?

Power banks are traditionally labeled by their cell capacity in milliamp-hours at the cell's nominal voltage (usually 3.6–3.85 V). To convert: Wh = mAh × voltage ÷ 1,000, so a 20,000 mAh bank holds roughly 72–77 Wh. Expect to get about 60–70% of the rated mAh into your phone because of conversion losses.

Can I bring a power bank or power station on a plane?

In the US, the FAA allows lithium batteries up to 100 Wh in carry-on bags without approval, and 100–160 Wh with airline approval. Spare lithium batteries aren't allowed in checked luggage. Most power banks up to about 26,800 mAh fall under 100 Wh; portable power stations are almost always too large to fly. Check your airline's current rules before traveling.

Does battery chemistry matter?

Most newer power stations use LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) cells, which are typically rated for 3,000+ charge cycles and are more thermally stable. Older or lighter models may use NMC cells, which are lighter for the same capacity but usually rated for fewer cycles. If you'll cycle the unit often, LiFePO4 is generally worth it.
